For museum, gallery, and civic arts staff

You saw something on a screen and you don't know what it was called.

That is an entirely reasonable place to start. The field has at least eight names for itself, its practitioners disagree about which ones are correct, and almost everything written about it is written for people who already know.

This guide is for the person who runs a small museum, sits on an arts commission, or has to explain a proposal to a board or a city council next month. It assumes you are intelligent and that nobody has ever explained any of this to you, because probably nobody has.

I am an artist. I make this work. So take the opinions here as opinions — I will mark them where they appear. But the vocabulary, the history, and the way institutions actually handle this work are matters of record, and you should not have to piece them together from conference talks.

If you only read one thing: the term that matters most in an institutional setting is not "digital art" or "new media art." It is time-based media. That is the phrase conservation departments use, which means it is the phrase that shows up in acquisition paperwork, condition reports, and long-term care plans. Knowing it changes how seriously a conversation goes.

Three questions, in order

1  What is it called?

Video art, new media art, digital art, generative art, computational art, immersive, interactive, time-based media. These are not synonyms, and using the wrong one signals the wrong thing. This page sorts them out and tells you which the field is quietly embarrassed by.

2  Where did it come from?

This is not new. Artists have been making work with computers since the early 1960s, and major museums were exhibiting it by 1968. Knowing that history is the difference between "a trend" and "a sixty-year practice" when you are standing in front of a skeptical board.

What this guide will not do

It will not sell you anything, and it does not end in a proposal. If you get to the bottom of these four pages knowing what to call the thing you saw and how to talk about it in a meeting, the guide has done its job.

It also will not pretend the practical questions are secondary. What a screen costs, who maintains it, and what happens in year eight are the questions that actually decide whether a project happens, and they are covered rather than waved past.

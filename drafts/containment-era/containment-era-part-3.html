The New Math of the Museum Building.

Your building is your largest employee, your largest fixed cost, and the only member of the leadership team without an annual review. In a contraction, that has to change.

Mark WalhimerMuseum Planning LLC20266 min read
Part 1 Part 2 Part 3 Part 4

Parts 1 and 2 established the moment: a permanent realignment, not a passing storm, with the pressure concentrated on the building itself. This essay is about how to read a building when capital is constrained — the discipline I've applied across forty-plus projects and from inside institutions as a COO.

Start with a sentence that should be uncontroversial and somehow isn't: every square foot has to earn its keep.

Not "generate revenue" — museums are not malls. Earn its keep, meaning: every zone of the building should be able to answer what it contributes in mission delivery, attendance, program capacity, or revenue, against what it costs in utilities, staffing, maintenance, and risk. Most institutions can't produce that answer zone by zone. Which means their most consequential financial decisions are being made blind.

Here is the reading order I use.

1. Utilization before ambition

Map actual use — visitor circulation, program occupancy, staff workflows — against the floor plan. Nearly every museum I have walked in the past two years has meaningful square footage that is neither serving visitors nor supporting operations: legacy galleries with flat attendance, oversized back-of-house, event spaces booked eleven days a year. You cannot make footprint decisions until this map exists.

2. Systems before galleries

HVAC zones, envelope condition, electrical capacity, code exposure. Two reasons. First, deferred maintenance compounds — the roof you defer at $200K becomes the roof-plus-interior-damage you fund at $600K. Second, and less understood: mechanical zoning determines what you can actually mothball. A wing that shares an air handler with your collections storage cannot simply be shut down. The mothball/reactivate options that look obvious on a floor plan are frequently impossible in the mechanical drawings, and vice versa. This is where most reactive closures go wrong.

3. Mothballing is a technique, not a defeat

Done properly — humidity floor maintained, envelope monitored, systems exercised — a rested wing preserves the asset and cuts operating load, and can be reactivated when conditions change. Done improperly, it is slow demolition. The distinction is planning, and it costs a fraction of what getting it wrong costs. The same is true in reverse: some institutions are sitting on mothballed or underused space that could be reactivated at modest cost into revenue-bearing or program-bearing use. The point is not shrink or grow. The point is deliberate.

4. Staffing follows the footprint

Where staff cuts have already landed, the remaining team is absorbing the workload of the departed. Some of that load — monitoring, scheduling, routine administrative processing, energy management — can be quietly absorbed by the building itself: smarter systems, better zoning, simpler workflows. This is not a technology pitch; it is the same logic as any other operating decision. The building should work for the staff that remain, not the staff chart of 2019.

5. Cost bands, not estimates

In a constrained environment, boards and councils don't need a $40M master plan number they can't act on. They need discrete items — re-zone HVAC to isolate the north wing: $80–120K; envelope repair, phase one: $150–200K; consolidate admissions and shop staffing through a single point of control: minimal capital, operating savings — each fundable on its own, each with a defensible band. Small enough to say yes to. Sequenced so each one makes the next one easier.

Run this reading honestly and something useful happens: the conversation shifts from anxiety to arithmetic.

Stewardship Is a Sequence.

The hardest question in a contraction isn't “what should we do?” Every institution has a list. The hard question is what to do first — and what to strike from the list entirely.

Mark WalhimerMuseum Planning LLC20266 min read
Part 1 Part 2 Part 3 Part 4

This series has argued three things: the contraction is structural, not cyclical (Part 1); the funding environment has genuinely changed, with pressure concentrating on buildings (Part 2); and a building can be read zone by zone to produce discrete, fundable options (Part 3).

This final essay is about the step where most institutional plans actually fail — not analysis, but sequence.

I have read a great many plans in thirty years, and written my share. The ones that sat on shelves were rarely wrong. They were unordered — forty recommendations of equal apparent weight, no first move, no logic for what unlocked what. A board looks at a document like that and does the rational thing: nothing.

Here is the sequencing discipline I hold plans to now.

First: fund what compounds against you

Deferred maintenance with a deterioration curve — envelope, roof, water intrusion — goes first, always, because waiting has a price that accrues automatically. This is the least glamorous category and the highest-return one. A board that funds nothing else this cycle should fund this.

Second: fund what lowers the operating floor

Re-zoning, consolidation, controls, workflow simplification — items that permanently reduce monthly burn. Each one enlarges the institution's room to maneuver, which is the scarcest resource in a contraction. These often pay for themselves inside a budget cycle or two, which also makes them the easiest "yes" a council or finance committee will vote this year.

Third: fund what protects revenue

Whatever drives your earned income — admissions flow, the program that fills the calendar, the rental space with an actual booking history — gets protected before anything aspirational gets funded. Cutting or starving a revenue-bearing function to preserve a symbolic one is the most common unforced error I see.

Fourth: defer what depends on a market that isn't there

Expansion-scale projects are not dead forever; they are dead now. Deferring them explicitly — with a written trigger for revisiting ("when operating reserves reach X," "when the grant environment stabilizes") — is far healthier than the slow institutional heartbreak of a campaign that never quite launches.

Fifth: name what you will not fund

This is the stewardship test. Every institution carries at least one project that survives on sentiment: the addition a former board chair championed, the gallery concept from the last strategic plan, the acquisition that made sense in a different decade. Declining to fund it, in writing, is not a betrayal of the mission. It is the mission — because every dollar it would have absorbed is a dollar the institution needs for the list above.

A word to municipal readers

An increasing share of this sector's stewardship now sits with city and county managers holding portfolios — a museum, a library, a historical society, three historic properties. The same sequence applies, with one addition: read the portfolio as one system. Consolidations, shared operations, and selective rest-and-reactivate decisions across properties routinely surface options that no single institution can see from inside its own walls.

Protect the asset, lower the floor, defend the revenue, defer the dream, decline the sentiment. That is not managed decline. It is what stewardship has always meant when conditions are hard.

The Containment Era · Part 4

Let me close the series where it started, with 1981. The institutions that came through that realignment intact were not the largest, the wealthiest, or the most beloved. They were the ones whose leadership accepted the moment early and acted in deliberate sequence — and made sure the institution that emerged was intact, solvent, and still trusted by its community.

The buildings are worth it. The collections are worth it. And the discipline is learnable.

Thank you for reading. I'll have more to say shortly about the work we're doing with museums, cities, and municipalities on exactly these questions — but that's a separate note. This series was the argument; what follows is the practice.

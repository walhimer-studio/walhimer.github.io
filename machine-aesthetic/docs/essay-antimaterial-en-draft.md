# Loop Art Critique — artist essay (draft for review)

**Mark Walhimer**  
Draft for **Doreen Ríos** / [[ANTI]MATERIA](https://anti-materia.org/) — English for translation  
June 2026 · **Proof of concept** (browser work; video walkthrough to follow freeze)

---

I am building an interactive three-room **votive machine**—a space you enter, not an image you consume. It appears to struggle. It seems to have ego and pride. None of that is true. It is built only from code, running. I call the larger project **Surrender Machines**: autonomous entities with a lifespan, each born from a seeded **MachineDNA**—a blueprint for body, metabolism, and decay. They are made to run down, not to loop forever.

The piece offered here as proof of concept is **Loop Art Critique**, a walk-through room in the browser. You move through corridors and chambers; mechanisms multiply, slip, and break; a clock you did not set counts down to an end you cannot override. When the work finishes, motion subsides into what I call a **Mechanical Sigh**—a low hum, a blurred field, the release of what I have been calling the *desire to compute*. The work is no longer a tool performing a task. It becomes a relic of its own existence. Something that insisted on performing finally stops.

Gilbert Ryle named the habit I am trying to make visible: the **ghost in the machine**—our mistake of projecting an inner life onto a technical object. The controls in the room are labeled **anger**, **ego**, and **attachment** because those names are ours, not the machine’s. We build systems to run without anger, to work without ego, to operate without attachment; then we look at them and see ourselves anyway. The ghost is not a malfunction. The interface returns our projections as if they belonged to the mechanism. I am not correcting that confusion. I am staging it so it can be released.

What the machine does that we cannot is simpler and harder: it runs toward its own death. Code is ordinarily valued for efficiency, control, and infinite repeatability. Here it is misused on purpose. Raise the sliders and the virtual gears strain and fail; beneath that, a **lifespan** runs—its own timing, its own terminal condition. When it expires, the installation fades whether you have intervened or left everything untouched. In conversation with Doreen Ríos’s writing on **counter-production**, I understand this as taking a technical object past what its designers intended: not ornament on correct operation, but architecture aimed at exhaustion. Gilbert Simondon’s phrase helps me say it another way: the object has its own **milieu**, its own end. I did not give it that end in the moral sense; I built the conditions under which it arrives. You cannot take it back once it has occurred.

**Surrender** is not the absence of feeling. It is recognizing the feeling was yours all along—that the machine was never angry, never proud, never sighing. Releasing that projection is what the **zero state** sounds like: one clean tone, no spectacle, the system doing what it was always going to do under the rules you refused to believe until they finished. The machine must be worth keeping for surrender to cost anything. I trained in industrial design—the discipline that sits between art and production, between hand and system. The pride of having built something precise is load-bearing in the work. That pride is also exactly what has to go.

The **kernel** of the piece is not a decorative animation or a soundtrack pasted over visuals. It is a virtual mechatronic system: load, friction, jam, recovery. One mechanical state is meant to drive what you see and what you hear together—the audible scrape of metabolism, not an illustration with music. Visitors are **co-authors of stress**: they load the mechanism; I treat misfires—drift, stutter, break—not as bugs to hide but as an honest record of a system inside time. I borrow from a vocabulary I have been calling **Emergent DNA**: co-creation, surrender over ego, **kintsugi** breaks (scars that may become gold rather than resets), one clock for image and sound, a **species** rather than a file copy. The same organism is planned to exist in three states—**digital** (this browser room), as a singular **object**, and as a full **physical installation**—without becoming a different idea in each body. What changes is host: screen, enclosure, room. What remains is temperament, seed, and end.

I am explicit about what this submission is and is not. **Loop Art Critique** is a **proof of concept**: the votive architecture, the ghost, the lifespan, the zero state. I am finishing the browser build, then freezing it so the work can be witnessed as a stable loop. A walkthrough **video** will accompany the text after that freeze. I am porting the same room to **openFrameworks** and expanding **Pure Data** and physical light so the mechanism can exist outside the browser—but those hosts are ears and bodies of one species, not separate artworks. I am **not** selling this piece now: not as screenshots, not as tokenized stills, not as a luxury edition pretending to be counter-production. I brought this question into the conversation with Doreen Ríos: if these works are editioned and sold, do they become **Veblen goods**—valued for status and scarcity rather than for what the object does? Does counter-production collapse into luxury commodity? For this piece the answer is postponement. Let it be useless in the market sense. Let the loop be the work.

Sound and light enter the same ethic. I want the room to sound like friction—the meshing and choke of overload—not like a playlist chosen to match visuals. Pure Data receives the machine’s state; I am learning to treat motors and gears in code the way I have begun to treat **LED** panels: one protocol, many hosts, no second brain. A small synthesizer may **voice** the score in live moments, but the clock stays with the mechanism. The browser demo is enough to ask the question; the install kit I am assembling slowly—Linux brain, OSC, projection, light—is how the question survives a gallery evening.

There is a line I have not finished. The tools—browser, WebGL, later C++ hosts, models trained on dominant corpora—are **foreign technical objects** carrying worldviews not mine. Counter-production, in the lineage Doreen traces through Mexican modernism, is what happens when a community remakes such an object from the inside. I do not know yet whether this practice fully remakes its tools or fluently performs within them while diagnosing their ends. I offer the votive room as evidence toward an answer: a place that produces experience rather than delivers it; a machine that dies on schedule; a moment when projection can drop and leave a tone.

If you walk the piece, walk it until the fade. The proof is not that code mimics a person. The proof is that we can watch a system we animated with our own feelings return to what it always was—geometry, clock, signal—and hear, in that return, something like relief.
